01

Ocean zones

Oceanographers divide the ocean into zones by depth and light, and life changes dramatically from one to the next.

The sunlight zone reaches down to about 200 metres and supports most marine plants and the majority of visible sea life. Below that, the twilight zone gets very little light. Beyond about 1,000 metres, the midnight zone is in permanent darkness, home to creatures that make their own light through bioluminescence.

02

Who lives there

The ocean holds more known species than any other habitat on Earth, spread across very different kinds of animals.

Coral reefs

Built by tiny animals called coral polyps, reefs support roughly a quarter of all marine species despite covering under 1% of the ocean floor.

Marine mammals

Whales, dolphins, and seals breathe air but spend most or all of their lives in the water.

Fish

The largest group of ocean vertebrates, from tiny gobies to the whale shark, the largest fish alive.

Deep-sea life

Anglerfish, giant squid, and tube worms survive extreme pressure, cold, and total darkness far from the surface.

03

Exploring the deep

Humans have only mapped a small fraction of the ocean floor in detail — we know the surface of the Moon better than our own seabed.

1872

The HMS Challenger expedition begins the first systematic study of the world's oceans.

1960

The bathyscaphe Trieste reaches the bottom of the Mariana Trench, the deepest known point in the ocean.

1977

Scientists discover hydrothermal vent ecosystems, thriving communities of life with no sunlight at all.

Today

Autonomous underwater vehicles and satellite mapping continue to reveal new details of the seafloor.

04

Why the ocean matters, and what threatens it

The ocean produces a large share of the oxygen we breathe, mostly from microscopic phytoplankton, and absorbs a significant portion of the carbon dioxide released into the atmosphere. It also moves heat around the planet through currents, regulating global climate.

  • Overfishing: Removing fish faster than populations can recover.
  • Plastic pollution: Waste that breaks down into microplastics found throughout the food chain.
  • Warming waters: Rising ocean temperatures stress coral reefs and shift species' ranges.
  • Acidification: Absorbed carbon dioxide lowers ocean pH, making it harder for shellfish and coral to build their skeletons.

Marine protected areas, fishing quotas, and reduced plastic use are among the most widely recommended tools for slowing damage to ocean ecosystems.

05

Glossary of ocean terms

Plankton
Tiny plants and animals that drift with ocean currents, forming the base of the marine food chain.
Bioluminescence
Light produced by a living organism, common among deep-sea creatures.
Current
A continuous, directed movement of ocean water, driven by wind, temperature, and salinity.
Hydrothermal vent
A fissure on the seafloor that releases geothermally heated water, supporting unique ecosystems.
Continental shelf
The shallow, gently sloping seabed surrounding a continent before it drops to the deep ocean floor.
Salinity
The measure of dissolved salt in ocean water.
